## 01 Introduction and scope

Hello. Can fandom activism take on the character of a peace movement? I explore this through Match A Million, a June 2020 campaign by ARMY, the fans of BTS.

This talk draws on my 2025 paper, co-authored with my advisor, Professor Tae Gyun Park. For today, I went back to the data to check its interpretation.

My main finding is that fandom pride and anti-racist solidarity appeared together.

## 02 Why Match A Million

In late May 2020, after George Floyd was killed by a police officer in Minneapolis, K-pop fans were already active online, flooding a Dallas police app with fan videos. On June 6, BTS and their agency donated one million dollars to Black Lives Matter, the movement against anti-Black racism. Within hours, One In An ARMY, a fan-run charity network, launched Match A Million: a campaign to match BTS's donation, dollar for dollar, with fans' own money. In about a day, fan donations passed one million dollars.

This campaign built on familiar fan practices: working together toward goals, such as pushing songs up the charts. At the same time, a 2021 study by Lee and Kao shows that Black ARMYs had already raised racism within the fandom. Match A Million brought these practices into contact with an anti-racist cause. For Hallyu research, this matters because attachment to a Korean artist became a route into a social issue in the United States.

Earlier studies looked at how ARMY organized the campaign. I focus on what fans said, with three research questions.

First, what discourse dominated at the start? Second, how did fandom pride and anti-racist solidarity appear together? Third, how did the mix of discourse change over the month? These questions organize the three findings.

## 03 Analytical perspectives

I use three perspectives to connect these questions to the analysis.

First, research on fandom activism helps me read internal cohesion: attachment, shared identity and shared achievement. It directs attention to how fans describe their bonds and goals.

Second, Bennett and Segerberg's connective action helps explain participation through personal acts that are easy to share. Here, one fan promises a dollar to Black Lives Matter for every like and retweet.

Third, Galtung's positive peace includes reducing structural violence such as racism. I use it to ask when the language goes beyond fandom achievement to address discrimination.

Together, these perspectives distinguish the bonds within the fandom, the way fans participate, and the public cause they support.

## 04 Data and method

I collected 628 tweets from the first month of the campaign, June 6 to July 6, 2020, and analyzed the 459 relevant English-language tweets.

I built the codes from theory and close reading, and one tweet could carry several codes, so I could see which appeared together. A keyword pass suggested codes first, and then I checked every tweet by hand.

## 05 Coding criteria

To distinguish fandom activity from a peace-oriented character, I asked two questions of each expression.

Is it directed toward bonds inside the fandom, or toward a public issue beyond it? And does it justify action through affection and achievement, or through a challenge to discrimination?

Talk of the first kind I call fandom-oriented. It has five codes; the most common is Relationship Emphasis, affection for BTS and fellow fans. Talk of the second kind I call peace-oriented, with three codes. The key one is Solidarity Expression, explicit support for a cause such as Black Lives Matter. A bare hashtag alone did not count. The full codebook with counts is in Appendix A1.

## 06 Early discourse

The first finding is about how the campaign took off. The graph counts the codes day by day: purple for fandom-oriented, blue for peace-oriented; the legend keeps the paper's older labels. In the first four days, fandom codes appeared about three times as often.

The tweets on the right show the mood: one counts down the last few thousand dollars to the goal, and the other says army are the CEOs of fandoms.

So the campaign took off on shared achievement and pride.

## 07 Cohesion and solidarity together

The second finding is that pride and solidarity appeared together. The figure on the left maps which codes appeared in the same tweet. Each circle is one code, and a thicker line means two codes appeared together more often. 168 of 459 tweets, about a third, mixed both kinds of talk. The tweet on the right does both: it thanks fellow fans, including for valuing Black lives.

Does love for the group lead fans to the cause? The thick line between Relationship Emphasis and Solidarity Expression seems to say so, and my paper read it that way. But both codes are common, so a thick line alone cannot establish that link. Affection alone was not associated with more explicit solidarity. Mentioning BTS's donation was: about four in ten of those tweets expressed solidarity, compared with one in four of the rest.

In these tweets, explicit solidarity was more closely associated with the artist's example than with affection alone.

## 08 The agenda of solidarity

Most solidarity tweets were short, often “I donated” with a hashtag. Only about thirty explained the cause in their own words, like the one on the left, proud of fans “who never stopped speaking of the justice we search.”

On the right, some fans stressed that they were giving before BTS did. So the artist was not the only route.

## 09 Early and late periods

The third finding is about change over the month. The figure on the left shows each day's share of peace-oriented codes in blue. It jumps around because later days have few tweets. On the right, I counted tweets, comparing the opening period with the final part of the month. The share about issues beyond the fandom rose from 40 to 64 percent. This broad category includes external recognition, such as media coverage, as well as explicit solidarity.

Does this mean the campaign turned into a peace movement? Total tweets fell from 374 to 58. Tweets beyond the fandom fell from 149 to 37, while all other tweets fell from 225 to 21. Both kinds of talk declined, but talk about public issues declined less sharply. That is why its share rose. This is a change in the mix of tweets, not evidence that individual fans changed.

## 10 Scope and limitations

What can these findings not show? Lasting participation, private motives, or Korean-language discourse. I was also the only coder, so the next step is to have another researcher code the same tweets separately and check how often we agree.

## 11 Examples explored after publication

To see whether this connection appears elsewhere, let me briefly turn to two later campaigns.

In August 2026, REMINE, the fandom of the girl group RESCENE, donated about 112.5 million won for flood relief in Geoje, the hometown of the group's leader, Woni.

Kpop4Planet, a fan climate group mostly in Indonesia, challenged Hana Bank, a Korean bank, over loans to coal-powered nickel processing in Indonesia.

These are different fandoms, so they cannot show continuity from 2020. But in both, the cause came in through K-pop artists: a member's hometown, and the bank's ambassador, G-Dragon.

## 12 The next research question

So the next question is this: under what conditions does fandom cohesion sustain engagement with a social problem?

The context I outlined suggests two candidate conditions: a clearly named problem and the voices of affected people within the fandom. And One In An ARMY, the group behind the campaign, still runs campaigns in 2026, but the cause changes each time, framed around a member. This shows organizational continuity, but not necessarily sustained engagement with the same issue.

Next, I plan to test these conditions with fan interviews, longer observation, and independent recoding, applied to later campaigns.

## 13 Connection to the Forum

The Forum asks about Hallyu along two axes: what Korea wishes to project, and what people abroad do with their attachment to it.

My findings speak to the receiver side. They suggest that we should ask which public causes fans engage with, and how, rather than assume that their actions follow Korea's agenda. This is one possible channel across borders, not Hallyu's effects in general.

## 14 Conclusion

Let me close with three points.

First, a peace-oriented character, yes, in a modest sense. Looking specifically at explicit solidarity or human-rights language, about three in ten tweets used it, and nearly nine in ten of those also carried fandom-oriented talk.

Second, a peace movement, not yet shown. The question becomes what would turn this connection into lasting engagement.

Third, when does fandom cohesion last? This case shows how fandom attachment, an artist's public action, and fan organizing came together around a public issue. What remains to be tested is when that connection lasts.

Thank you.
